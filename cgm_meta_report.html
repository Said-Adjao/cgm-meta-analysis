Continuous Glucose Monitoring vs. Blood Glucose Monitoring in Non-Insulin-Treated Type 2 Diabetes

A reproducible meta-analysis of HbA1c outcomes

Author

Said Adjao

Published

June 18, 2026

1 Summary

This report synthesizes randomized evidence on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) versus blood glucose monitoring (BGM) for glycaemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes not treated with insulin — a population for which CGM coverage is frequently restricted. Pooling 8 trials, CGM was associated with a mean HbA1c reduction of -0.42% (95% CI -0.59 to -0.25), with no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The estimate is consistent with a recently published meta-analysis, supporting a modest but reliable benefit in this population.

2 Background

Most clinical-trial evidence and payer coverage for CGM in type 2 diabetes derives from insulin-treated populations. Whether the benefit extends to non-insulin-treated patients — the larger group, and the one typically excluded from CGM reimbursement — is a distinct question with direct cost and coverage implications. This analysis synthesizes the randomized evidence specific to that population.

3 Methods

3.1 Eligibility criteria

Randomized controlled trials were eligible if they enrolled adults with type 2 diabetes treated exclusively with non-insulin therapies, compared any CGM modality (intermittently scanned, real-time, or professional) against BGM or usual care, and reported HbA1c as an outcome. Trials enrolling any insulin-treated participants were excluded.

3.2 Information sources and study selection

The trial set was harvested from a 2025 systematic review by Aronson and colleagues that applied the same non-insulin-only inclusion criterion, providing a transparent and reproducible starting point. Eight trials met criteria.

3.3 Data extraction

HbA1c effect estimates were obtained by two routes. For two trials, the between-group mean difference and its dispersion were extracted directly from the primary publications. For the remaining six, the standard error was back-calculated from the reported mean difference and its p-value or confidence interval — a standard technique where standard errors are not printed directly. The extraction route for each trial is shown in the results table. One trial reporting HbA1c in IFCC units (mmol/mol) was converted to DCCT/NGSP units (%) prior to pooling.

3.4 Statistical analysis

Effects were pooled using an inverse-variance random-effects model (mean difference scale), implemented with the meta package in R. Random effects were chosen a priori because the trials differed in CGM modality, follow-up duration, and co-interventions, making a single common true effect implausible. Heterogeneity was quantified with I².

Pooling all 8 trials, CGM was associated with a significantly greater HbA1c reduction than BGM (mean difference -0.42%; 95% CI -0.59 to -0.25). Between-study heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0%), though this should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of small trials.

4.1 Validation

This pooled estimate is consistent with the published benchmark (−0.37%; 95% CI −0.49 to −0.24), reproducing a peer-reviewed result from the primary data. Minor differences are attributable to the standard-error derivation and missing-data imputation approaches.

5 Limitations

Several limitations temper confidence in the pooled estimate. The evidence base is small (8 trials, most with modest sample sizes), limiting the precision of the heterogeneity estimate; an I² of 0% in this setting reflects absence of detected heterogeneity rather than strong evidence of consistency. All trials were open-label, raising the possibility of performance and detection bias. Several interventions bundled CGM with structured education or lifestyle programmes, so the pooled effect reflects CGM-plus-support rather than the device in isolation. Finally, six of eight effect estimates were back-calculated; these should be verified against the published forest plot before any formal use.
